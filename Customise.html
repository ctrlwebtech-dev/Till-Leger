Topic 5: Adapting and customising information systems

The Software Challenge in Retail

A small retailer should follow crucial steps when creating software; configure first, add bolt-ons next, connect systems, and change code last. A settings change costs nothing when the vendor upgrades, whereas custom code and new workflows must be checked after every new version, and often rebuilt. Software packages are created based on the general concept of retail business. The difference between standard software capabilities and real workflow processes is called software "misfit".

In South African retail, misfits are normally associated with managing lay-by sales, cash/credit card transactions, calculating loyalty points and VAT compliance for SARS. Apart from software-related issues, employee nostalgia and managers' attachment to old-fashioned procedures often prompt unnecessary customization requirements. Retailers should understand whether the lack of software capabilities is real or just a resistance to change in procedures.

The Spectrum of Tailoring Choices

There are nine levels of customization of a packaged system ranging from simple adjustments in settings to rewriting the code supplied by the vendor. Upgrade risk ratings were evaluated by us for a small retailer. The first four require little work of a programmer. The remaining five always require a programmer, and a small store typically doesn't have one. Each level of customization increases support costs as each vendor upgrade must be tested and re-written. The issue of customization is considered a choice between altering the system and altering the process. In case of a store without IT department altering the process is obviously cheaper.

API Customisation

An application programming interface (API) is a published set of rules that lets one program request data from another or send data to it. APIs act as a main route for systems to share data and services, they also make SMEs more efficient by linking systems that used to run separately. In a store, the cloud POS sends daily sales to the accounting package, pushes stock levels to the online shop and pulls loyalty balances into the till. If the store switches accounting packages later, only the link changes.

SDLC To Manage Change

We use six phases of the systems development life cycle (SDLC) to keep the change under control.

  1. Phase 1. Identifying the main operational issues (such as stock counts not matching POS till totals), defining a realistic budget for implementation, and setting realistic go-live dates.

  2. Phase 2. Analysis: Analysis of how standard software capabilities fit against store needs, where each capability is classified as either a standard fit, a configuration gap, or an external API integration gap.

  3. Phase 3. Design: Fixing the identified gaps with the least risky solution from the spectrum (preferably configuration and API, not custom coding).

  4. Phase 4. Testing: Checking that stock levels and accounting totals match as well as conducting test transactions, including standard sales, customer returns, lay-bys, and offline sales under load-shedding conditions.

  5. Phase 5. Implementation: Importing clean product and customer data, and training staff. As well as conducting operations such as running the old and new systems side by side for one to two weeks, while the manager compares totals every day. This method is preferred over a pilot or a direct cut-over as a shop with two or three tills learns little from a pilot, and a cut-over leaves nothing to fall back on if the totals go wrong. A short spell of double capturing is cheaper than untangling a failed changeover.

  6. Phase 6. Maintenance: Reading the vendor’s update notes, checking each API link after every update and cancelling add-ons that aren’t in use. With no IT staff, the owner should choose the vendor’s support as carefully as the software.

Power in the South African Context

South African SMEs balance the low cost of SaaS ERP implementation with reliance on the vendor and the Internet. It has been shown that SMEs make use of only a fraction of ERP functionality they purchase, and that companies making use of more of them tend to enjoy competitive advantage. The retailer needs to make full use of the standard features before considering any customization. POS adoption in Nigerian SMEs have been connected to availability of power and Internet connections. The load shedding affects South African shops in the same way. It requires POS that can work offline and sync later.

Design and Pedagogy of Reasoning

It is quite common for small businesses to fall into the trap of over-customizing an existing but inflexible software package or trying to develop a completely customized software solution on their own. To make the process of adapting their systems easier for the owners, this topic was organized according to the System Development Life Cycle (SDLC). Through the discussion of an API-based checklist, retail managers are shown how they can customize the existing POS and ecommerce platforms to match their own processes.
